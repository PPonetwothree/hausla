Hearing that you, or someone you love, has cancer changes the ground under your feet. This guide is about the feelings that often follow, and some gentle ways through them.

**You don't need to read all of it now.** If today is very hard, read only the next section, and come back later.

## All of these feelings are normal

Cancer can bring up emotions you're not used to dealing with, and make existing feelings stronger. They can change from day to day, hour to hour, even minute to minute. There is no right way to feel.

The way you were brought up, and your family's values, often shape how you cope. Some people feel they must stay strong to protect their family. Some turn to loved ones, or to others who have had cancer. Some ask a counsellor for help. Some turn to their faith. Do what is right for you, and try not to compare yourself with others.

If you are a family member, you may be feeling many of the same things.

## Feeling overwhelmed

When you first learn you have cancer, it is normal to feel that life is out of control. You may be wondering whether you will live. Your routine is suddenly full of hospital visits. Doctors use words you don't understand.

What can help:

- **Learn a little at a time.** Most people feel more in control as they understand more about their illness and its treatment. Ask your doctor questions, and say so when you don't understand. Our [glossary](/glossary) explains common words.
- **Take a break from cancer** when you can. If you have the energy, do something you enjoy: music, reading, prayer, a walk, time with family.

## Fear and worry

It is frightening to hear the word cancer. People often worry about pain, about how treatment will make them feel or look, about their family, about money and work, and about dying.

Some fears come from stories, rumours or wrong information. **Most people feel less afraid once they know the facts about their own situation**, so ask your care team, and be careful with advice from people who aren't your doctors.

## Stress and anxiety

It's normal to feel stressed about all the changes at once: costs, travel to the hospital, work, daily routines, where to find support. It helps to raise these early, or to ask a family member to raise them for you:

- **For money worries**, talk to your care team and the hospital's patient welfare or social service department. See [help with treatment costs](/find-care/financial-help).
- **For emotional and practical support**, ask the hospital's social worker or nurse what help is available.

Anxiety can show up in the body: a racing heart, headaches, poor sleep, trouble eating or concentrating, feeling shaky or tight in the chest. **If you notice signs like these, tell your doctor.** They are common with stress, but the doctor will want to check they aren't caused by medicines or treatment.

## Anger

It is very normal to ask "Why me?", and to feel angry at the illness, at doctors, at healthy friends, at family, even at God. Anger often hides other feelings that are hard to show: fear, panic, frustration, helplessness.

You don't have to pretend everything is fine. Keeping anger inside isn't good for you, but constant anger doesn't feel good either. Talking to someone you trust can help.

## Sadness, and when it might be depression

Many people feel sad: a sense of loss of their health, and of the life they had before. For many, this eases with time.

For some, the sadness gets stronger and gets in the way of daily life. This may be **depression, which is a medical condition and can be treated.** Talk to your doctor if, for **more than two weeks**, you notice signs such as:

- sadness that doesn't go away, or feeling numb, helpless or hopeless
- losing interest in things you used to enjoy, including food or family
- crying for long periods, or many times a day
- trouble sleeping, or sleeping too much; tiredness that doesn't go away
- finding it hard to concentrate

Some of these can also be caused by physical problems, which is another reason to tell your doctor. You don't have to manage these feelings alone.

> **If you have thoughts of hurting yourself or of suicide, please reach out now.** Call **Tele-MANAS on 14416**, free, at any time, or **112** in an emergency.

## Loneliness

People with cancer often feel alone, even among family. Some friends find it hard to cope and stop calling. You may feel too unwell to do what you used to, or feel that no one understands.

Look for support in whatever way suits you: a close friend or family member, a counsellor, a religious or spiritual community, or other people who have had cancer.

## Guilt

Many people with cancer feel guilty: for upsetting family, for being a "burden", for missing work, for the cost of treatment, or because they think their lifestyle caused it.

**Having cancer is not your fault.** No one knows exactly why some people get cancer and others don't. Sharing these feelings with someone you trust, or a counsellor, can help.

## Hope, and small good moments

Once the first shock passes and treatment has a routine, many people find a sense of hope returns. Many people live with cancer and beyond it.

Hope doesn't mean pretending to be cheerful, and how you feel does not decide how your illness goes. But hope, and small good moments, can make the days easier:

- Keep a routine where you can, even if it changes.
- Keep doing the things you like, as much as you're able.
- Notice what makes you smile: tea with someone you love, time with a child, a favourite song, a place that means something to you.
- Draw on your faith or spiritual beliefs, if they help you.

## What to do this week

You don't have to do everything at once. If you can, choose one or two:

1. Ask the doctor to write down the diagnosis and the next step.
2. Put all reports in one folder. See [papers to bring](/find-care/coming-to-delhi#papers).
3. Tell one person you trust how you're feeling.
4. Ask the hospital about help with costs, early.
