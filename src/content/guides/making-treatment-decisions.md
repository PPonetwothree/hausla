Deciding on treatment can feel like being asked to choose in a language you've only just started learning. This guide won't tell you which treatment is right; only your doctors can advise on that. It explains the words, and gives you questions to ask so that the decision is one you understand.

## How treatment is chosen

There are many types of cancer treatment. Some people have only one. Most have a **combination**, for example surgery together with chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

The treatment a doctor recommends depends on the **type of cancer** and **how advanced it is** (see [stage and grade](/guides/understanding-cancer-basics#stage-how-far-the-cancer-has-spread)), along with other test results and the person's overall health. Doctors may also order tests on the tumour itself, sometimes called biomarker tests, to help choose a treatment.

This is why two people with cancer in the same organ can be given different treatment.

## The main types of treatment

| Treatment | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Surgery** | An operation in which a surgeon removes cancer from the body. |
| **Chemotherapy** | Medicines that kill cancer cells, or stop them growing. They also affect other fast-growing cells in the body. |
| **Radiotherapy** (radiation therapy) | High doses of radiation used to kill cancer cells and shrink tumours. |
| **Targeted therapy** | Medicines that target the specific changes in cancer cells that help them grow, divide and spread. |
| **Immunotherapy** | Treatment that helps your own immune system fight the cancer. |
| **Hormone therapy** | Slows or stops the growth of cancers that use hormones to grow, such as some breast and prostate cancers. |
| **Stem cell (bone marrow) transplant** | Restores blood-forming cells in people whose own have been destroyed by very high doses of chemotherapy or radiotherapy. |

Not every treatment is used for every cancer, and not every hospital offers every treatment.

## Questions to ask before you decide

Take a notebook and, if you can, another person. It is fine to ask the doctor to slow down or to repeat something.

**About the treatment**

- What are the treatments for my type and stage of cancer?
- What are the benefits and risks of each?
- Which do you recommend, and why do you think it is best for me?
- When does treatment need to start?
- How will we know if it is working?

**About what will happen**

- Where will treatment be given? Will I need to stay in hospital, and for how long?
- How many sessions will there be, and how long does each take?
- Should someone come with me?
- Will I be able to work, study or manage at home?

**About side effects**

- What side effects are possible, during and between sessions?
- Which side effects should I contact you about straight away?
- Are any side effects lasting?
- Could this treatment affect my ability to have children?
- How can side effects be prevented or treated?

**About cost and place** (for families in India)

- What will this cost here, roughly, and what does that include?
- Is it covered by Ayushman Bharat or another scheme at this hospital? See [help with treatment costs](/find-care/financial-help).
- Can any part of the treatment be given closer to home?

## Getting a second opinion

Before starting treatment, you may want another specialist to look at your case. This is called a **second opinion**, and it is very common.

The second doctor may agree with the plan, or suggest changes or another approach. Either way, you get more information, answers to your questions, and more confidence that you have explored your options.

Some people worry their doctor will be offended. Usually the opposite is true: most doctors welcome a second opinion.

**How to ask.** It can help to say that you are satisfied with your care but want to be sure you understand all your options. Involve your doctor, because the second doctor will need your records: reports, scans and biopsy results. Bring a family member for support if you can.

**A caution about time.** Ask your doctor how soon treatment needs to begin, so that a second opinion does not cause a delay that matters.

## Clinical trials

**Clinical trials** are research studies that test how well new medical approaches work in people, including new treatments and new ways of using existing ones. Many of today's treatments exist because of past trials.

You can ask your doctor: *"Would a clinical trial be right for me?"* Taking part is always your choice.

## Other remedies: tell your doctor

Many families also consider other remedies, such as herbal, ayurvedic or homeopathic medicines, special diets, supplements, or practices like yoga and meditation. It helps to know two words:

- **Complementary** means used **along with** the treatment your cancer doctors give. Some complementary practices, such as yoga, meditation and acupuncture, have been carefully studied and found to be generally safe, and may help with stress or side effects.
- **Alternative** means used **instead of** that treatment, for example a special diet in place of the medicines an oncologist prescribes. Far less research has been done on these.

Three things to remember:

1. **"Natural" does not mean safe.** Some products don't work, some can be harmful, and some can interfere with cancer treatment.
2. **Tell your doctor about everything you take**, including vitamins, herbs and home remedies. They ask so that your treatment works as it should, not to judge you.
3. **Ask before you start anything new**: Will this interfere with my treatment? Has it been shown to do what it claims?

Be very careful with anyone who promises a cure, asks you to stop your hospital treatment, or asks for large payments.

## Making the decision

- Ask how much time you have to decide. Often there is time to think, talk with family and ask more questions.
- Write down what matters most to you, and tell your doctor.
- It's all right to ask the same question twice.
- Once you decide, ask for the plan in writing.
