If you are helping someone you love through cancer, you are a **caregiver**, whether you are their child, parent, spouse, sibling, friend or neighbour. In many Indian families, caregiving is shared across relatives, and it often falls most heavily on one or two people. This guide is for them.

You don't need to read it all at once. Each section stands on its own.

## What caregiving means

Giving care can mean helping with medical needs and daily tasks: going to hospital visits, cooking, picking up medicines, handling paperwork. It also means helping your loved one cope with their feelings. Sometimes simply being there, someone to talk to, is what they need most.

While caring for someone, it is normal to put your own needs aside. But doing that for a long time is not good for your health, and if you become unwell, you may not be able to care for them. Looking after yourself is part of looking after them.

Caregiving may be a new role. You might be caring for a spouse who was always healthy, or a parent who always cared for you. Feeling confused and stressed is normal. Many caregivers say they learned as they went.

## Your feelings

There is no right way to feel. Some feelings that often come and go:

- **Sadness.** It's okay to feel sad. But if it lasts for more than two weeks and stops you from doing what you need to do, talk to a doctor.
- **Anger**, at yourself, at family members, even at the person you're caring for. Anger often comes from fear, panic or stress.
- **Grief** for the life you had before the illness, and for your loved one's health. Let yourself grieve these losses.
- **Guilt** that you aren't doing enough, or that you are healthy.
- **Loneliness**, even with many people around, because it can feel as if no one understands.

### What can help

- **Talk to someone** if your feelings get in the way of daily life: a family member, a friend, a religious or spiritual leader, a doctor, or the hospital's social worker or counsellor.
- **Forgive yourself.** Everyone makes mistakes when there is a lot on their mind. You are doing what you can.
- **Let yourself cry or be upset.** You don't have to pretend to be cheerful.
- **Let small things go** for now, and save your energy for what matters.
- **Don't take your loved one's anger personally.** People often direct their fear and stress at those closest to them.
- **Hope can change shape.** What you hope for may change over time, but you can always hope for comfort, peace and moments of joy.

If you ever feel you can't cope, you can call **Tele-MANAS on 14416**, free, at any time.

## Ask for help, sooner than you think

Many people who were once caregivers say they did too much on their own, and wish they had asked for help sooner. Things often get harder as treatment goes on.

Asking for help means you may stay healthier and have more energy. Your loved one may also feel less guilty about how much you are doing.

People often want to help but don't know how. Make a list, and give people specific jobs:

- standing in hospital queues, or staying overnight so you can sleep
- cooking, cleaning, shopping, looking after children or elders at home
- driving to appointments or the pharmacy
- finding information, such as how to apply for [financial help](/find-care/financial-help)
- being the **one person who updates everyone else**, for example in the family WhatsApp group, so you don't have to answer every call

Some people will say no. They may have their own problems, no time, or not know how to be around illness. That's not a reflection on you.

## Caring for yourself

Do something for yourself every day, however small: a phone call with a friend, a walk, music, prayer, a few quiet minutes with tea. Light activity like walking or stretching can make you feel less tired.

Caregivers commonly have trouble sleeping, headaches and anxiety. Try to:

- keep up with your own check-ups and medicines
- eat proper meals and rest when you can
- tell your own doctor about any new changes in your body

This sounds simple, and it is hard to do. That is exactly why it matters.

Many caregivers also find it helps to talk with other caregivers. The hospital's nurse or social worker may know of a support group.

## Going to hospital visits together

Your loved one may want you with them at appointments. This can be one of the most useful things you do.

**Before the visit**

- Know how to get there, and leave plenty of time for travel and queues.
- Write down the questions you want to ask, and anything you want to tell the doctor.
- Keep a folder of all reports and bring it every time. See [papers to bring](/find-care/coming-to-delhi#papers).
- Bring all the medicines, or a written list of their names and doses.

**During the visit**

- If you don't understand an answer, ask the question in a different way.
- Tell the doctor or nurse about your worries, and about any changes or new symptoms.
- Take notes, or ask whether you may record the conversation.
- Before you leave, make sure you know the next steps.

**Questions you might ask the care team**

- How can we prepare for treatment? How long will it take?
- Can I be with them during treatment?
- What side effects might there be?
- After treatment, what should we watch for, and when should we contact you?
- Who can help us with costs, insurance or government schemes?

### Pain: always speak up

Many caregivers are afraid to ask about pain, worried it means the cancer is getting worse, or believing pain just has to be accepted. That is not true. Pain can usually be managed during treatment, and people whose pain is managed can focus on healing and enjoy life more. Tell the doctor about any pain at every visit, and ask how and when pain medicines should be taken.

### Second opinions

Some people worry a doctor will be offended if they ask for a second opinion. Usually the opposite is true; most doctors welcome it. A second doctor may agree with the plan or suggest another approach. Either way, you have more information and can feel more confident about the choices you make.

## Family, partners and children

**With your partner or spouse,** stress is normal. Talk openly about how each of you is coping, make decisions together where you can, and make some time for things other than cancer. If closeness or intimacy has changed, talking about it gently, or with a counsellor, can help.

**With the wider family,** old tensions can become sharper under stress. Be as open and kind as you can, and ask someone else to handle updates when you're exhausted.

**When people give advice you didn't ask for,** remember they often don't know what else to say. You don't have to respond. Thank them and let it go.

### Talking to children

Children notice more than adults think. If they aren't told the truth, they often imagine something worse. They may feel confused, angry, guilty or scared, become clingy, or have trouble eating, sleeping or at school.

- **Tell them about the illness** in words they understand. Make sure they know nothing they did caused it, and that they cannot catch it.
- **Tell them their feelings are okay**, and that you will always love them.
- **Be honest, with love and hope.** Say that your loved one is getting good care and that you hope they will get better, but don't promise an outcome you aren't sure of.
- **Listen.** Ask what worries them. Younger children may show their feelings through drawing or play.
- **Stay close.** Eat meals together, ask about their day, call them when you're at the hospital.

## Remember

- Try to strike a balance each day.
- Your needs matter too.
- Rest, and accept help.

Many people say that caring for someone with cancer changed them: they discovered strengths they didn't know they had, and learned what matters most to them. Whatever happens, the care you give matters.
