This guide explains, in plain words, what cancer is and the words you are likely to see in reports and hear from doctors. It is general information. **Your own doctor is the right person to explain what your reports mean for you.**

## What cancer is

Our bodies are made of trillions of tiny cells. Normally, cells grow and divide to make new cells when the body needs them, and old or damaged cells die and are replaced.

**Cancer is a disease in which some of the body's cells grow uncontrollably and can spread to other parts of the body.** The orderly process breaks down: abnormal or damaged cells keep growing and dividing when they shouldn't.

These cells may form a lump of tissue called a **tumour**.

- A **malignant** tumour is cancer. It can grow into nearby tissue and can travel to other parts of the body.
- A **benign** tumour is not cancer. It does not spread into nearby tissue, and usually doesn't grow back once removed. Some benign tumours can still be large or cause serious problems, for example in the brain.

Not every cancer forms a lump. Blood cancers such as leukaemia generally do not form solid tumours.

## How cancer starts

Cancer is caused by changes in **genes**, the instructions inside cells that control how they grow and divide. These changes can happen because of:

- errors that happen naturally when cells divide
- damage from harmful substances, such as the chemicals in **tobacco smoke**
- changes inherited from parents

The body normally gets rid of damaged cells, but this ability weakens with age, which is one reason cancer is more common in older people.

Every person's cancer has its own combination of genetic changes. This is one reason two people with "the same" cancer may be treated differently. Some treatments are designed to target particular changes, which is why doctors sometimes order extra tests on the tumour.

**Cancer is not your fault, and it is not caught from someone else.**

## When cancer spreads

When cancer cells break away from where they started and form new tumours elsewhere, this is called **metastasis**, and the cancer is called **metastatic**.

Metastatic cancer keeps the name of where it started. For example, breast cancer that has spread to the lung is called **metastatic breast cancer**, not lung cancer, because the cells are still breast cancer cells.

## Types of cancer

There are more than 100 types of cancer. They are usually named after the part of the body where they start (breast cancer, lung cancer), or the type of cell they start in. Words you may hear include:

- **Carcinoma**: the most common type; starts in the cells that line the inside and outside surfaces of the body. **Adenocarcinoma** is a carcinoma that starts in cells that make fluids or mucus.
- **Sarcoma**: starts in bone or soft tissues such as muscle, fat or blood vessels.
- **Leukaemia**: starts in the blood-forming tissue of the bone marrow.
- **Lymphoma**: starts in lymphocytes, white blood cells that are part of the immune system.
- **Multiple myeloma**: starts in plasma cells, another kind of immune cell, in the bone marrow.
- **Melanoma**: starts in the cells that give skin its colour.

## Not every change is cancer

Doctors sometimes find tissue changes that are **not cancer** but are watched because they could become cancer if left alone. Your report may use words like **hyperplasia** or **dysplasia**, or **carcinoma in situ** ("stage 0"), where abnormal cells are present but have not spread into nearby tissue. Ask your doctor what any such word means for you.

## How cancer is diagnosed

There is no single test for cancer. Doctors usually start with your history and an examination, then may order:

- **Lab tests** of blood, urine or other fluids. An abnormal result is **not** a sure sign of cancer, and a normal result doesn't always rule it out, so lab tests are always read alongside other tests.
- **Imaging tests** (scans), such as X-rays, ultrasound, CT or MRI, to see inside the body.
- **A biopsy**, which in most cases is the only way to be certain.

### What a biopsy is

A **biopsy** removes a small sample of the abnormal tissue. It may be taken with a needle, through a thin tube with a camera (an **endoscope**) passed into the body, or during surgery.

A specialist doctor called a **pathologist** examines the sample under a microscope. Their findings go into a **pathology report** (often called the histopathology or "HPE" report). Keep it safe: it helps doctors decide which treatments might work.

If reports reach you before you can see the doctor, it's natural to read them and worry. Try to wait for the doctor to explain them; many words in reports sound frightening out of context.

## Stage: how far the cancer has spread

**Stage** describes how large the cancer is and whether, and how far, it has spread. It helps doctors understand how serious it is and plan treatment.

Many reports use the **TNM system**:

- **T** (tumour): the size and extent of the main tumour. T1 to T4: the higher the number, the larger it is or the more it has grown into nearby tissue.
- **N** (nodes): whether cancer is in nearby **lymph nodes**. N0 means none; N1 to N3 mean increasing numbers or spread.
- **M** (metastasis): whether it has spread to distant parts of the body. M0 means no; M1 means yes.

An **X** (as in TX, NX or MX) means that part couldn't be measured.

Doctors often group these into simpler stages:

| Stage | What it means |
|---|---|
| Stage 0 | Abnormal cells that have not spread into nearby tissue (carcinoma in situ). Not cancer, but may become cancer. |
| Stage I, II, III | Cancer is present. The higher the number, the larger it is and the more it has spread into nearby tissue. |
| Stage IV | The cancer has spread to distant parts of the body. |

Some cancers, such as blood cancers and brain tumours, use different staging systems.

A cancer **keeps the stage it was given at diagnosis**, even if it changes later; new information is added to the original stage.

## Grade: how abnormal the cells look

**Grade** is different from stage. It describes how abnormal the cancer cells look under the microscope. In general, the more normal the cells look, the more slowly the cancer tends to grow and spread.

| Grade | Report may say |
|---|---|
| Grade 1 (low) | Well differentiated: cells look close to normal |
| Grade 2 (intermediate) | Moderately differentiated |
| Grade 3 (high) | Poorly differentiated |
| Grade 4 (high) | Undifferentiated: cells look most abnormal |
| Grade X | Grade couldn't be assessed |

Grading systems differ between cancers.

## What stage and grade can, and can't, tell you

Stage and grade help doctors plan treatment. They describe the cancer, not the person, and they cannot tell you exactly what will happen to you. Your doctor will consider many things together: the type of cancer, its stage and grade, other test results, and your overall health.

**Questions you might ask:**

- What type of cancer is it, and where did it start?
- What stage and grade is it? What does that mean for us?
- Are more tests needed before treatment can be planned?
- Can we have copies of all the reports?
