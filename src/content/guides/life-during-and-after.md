The end of treatment is something people look forward to for months. When it comes, it often feels more complicated than expected. This guide is about that time: what's normal, what to ask your doctors, and how to live alongside the worry.

## When treatment ends

Getting used to life after treatment takes time. Many people feel relief, and also feel anxious, a little lost, or sad. It can feel like a roller coaster.

It can be hard when family and friends think you're ready to "move on" and you're not. You may still:

- feel tired and not want to do much
- be healing from treatment and its side effects
- feel nervous about seeing your oncologist less often
- worry that the cancer will come back
- feel unsure about how to move forward

None of this means something is wrong with you.

## Finding a "new normal"

People who have been through treatment often describe the first few months as a time of change. It is less about "getting back to normal" and more about finding out what is normal for you now.

Your new normal may include different plans or goals, changes in how you eat, new routines, scars, things that are harder to do than before, new sources of support, and feelings left over from all you've been through. You may see yourself differently, and others may treat you differently.

Give yourself time. Take it one day at a time.

## Follow-up care

### Ask for a follow-up care plan

When treatment ends, ask your oncologist or care team for:

1. **A treatment summary**: a written record of your diagnosis and the treatment you received.
2. **A follow-up care plan**: what check-ups and tests you need, how often, and what to watch for.

**Questions to ask**

- How long will it take to feel more like myself?
- Which doctor should I see for follow-up, and how often?
- What symptoms should I watch for?
- What tests will I need, and how often?
- What long-term effects might my treatment have?
- What records should I keep?
- What can I do to stay as healthy as possible?

### How often are follow-up visits?

Every person's schedule is different. It depends on the type of cancer, the treatment you had and your overall health. In general, visits are more frequent in the first two to three years after treatment and less frequent after that. **Follow the schedule your own doctor gives you.**

### Keep your records safe, for years

Keep your treatment summary and reports together in a folder, and keep them long-term. Your summary should include:

- the date of diagnosis and the type of cancer
- pathology reports describing the type and stage
- the places and dates of each treatment: surgeries, radiotherapy sites and amounts, and the names and doses of chemotherapy and other medicines
- key lab and scan reports
- signs and symptoms to watch for, and possible long-term effects

If you were treated far from home, for example in Delhi, this folder matters even more: it lets a doctor near your home understand exactly what was done. If you see more than one doctor, ask each to share their notes with the others, and carry your folder to every visit.

### What to tell the doctor at follow-up visits

Talk openly about any physical or emotional problems. Always mention:

- any symptoms, pain or concerns that are new or won't go away
- tiredness, trouble sleeping, changes in weight
- problems with bladder, bowel or sexual function
- trouble concentrating or memory changes
- anxiety, constant worry or low mood
- any new medicines, vitamins, herbal or other remedies you are taking

**A new symptom does not necessarily mean the cancer has come back.** It may be something your doctor can easily deal with.

Some treatments can cause problems months or years later, called **late effects**. Ask your doctor which ones to watch for. Don't wait for the next scheduled visit if something changes; contact your doctor.

## Living with the fear of it coming back

The most common fear after treatment is that the cancer will return. Scans and follow-up tests can bring a lot of anxiety. **This fear is normal, and it often lessens over time.** Even years later, a follow-up visit, an ache, someone else's illness, or the anniversary of your diagnosis can bring it back.

Things that can help:

- **Tell your care team about your fears.** The risk is different for every person. They can give you the facts for your situation.
- **Know that worrying about every ache is common.** Ask how long side effects may last, and tell them about any symptom that worries you.
- **Keep a notebook** of symptoms, feelings and questions, and bring it to visits.
- **Keep busy while waiting for results.** Spend time with family or friends, or do something that takes your mind off the wait.
- **Focus on what you can control**: keeping appointments, asking questions, a daily routine.
- **Talk to a counsellor** if the fear is more than you can handle. See [Building Mental Strength & Resilience](/guides/building-mental-strength).

## Looking after mind and body

- **Find ways to relax.** Relaxation exercises, meditation and yoga help many people with stress.
- **Talk to others**: family, friends, or other people who have had cancer. Ask the hospital's social worker about support groups.
- **Be active**, as your doctor advises. Moderate exercise such as walking can reduce anxiety and low mood.
- **Eat well.** Ask your doctor or a dietitian what is right for you.
- **Write your feelings down.** Many people find it helps them let go of worries.
- **Draw on faith or spirituality**, if it is a source of strength for you.
- **Give back, if you wish.** Some people find meaning in helping others going through the same thing.
- **Spend time with people**, doing things that aren't about cancer.

## If you are still in treatment

Much of this applies during treatment too: keeping records together, telling your team about every symptom, and looking after your mind as well as your body. Asking early what follow-up will look like can make the end of treatment less unsettling.
