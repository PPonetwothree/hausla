People facing cancer are often told to "be strong" or "stay positive". This guide takes a more honest view of strength: not pretending you're fine, but finding ways to cope, one day at a time, and getting help when you need it.

## What resilience really means

Resilience isn't the absence of fear or sadness. It is the slow work of adjusting to big changes: learning to live with difficult feelings and to solve the problems cancer brings.

Two things are important to say clearly:

- **Your mood does not decide how your illness goes.** You don't have to be cheerful for treatment to work, and a bad day is not a failure.
- **Coping well can still include crying, anger and fear.** Strength often looks like asking for help.

## Distress is common, and it's not weakness

Almost everyone living with cancer feels distress at some point. It can range from sadness and worry to more serious problems such as depression, panic, or feeling cut off from others or from one's faith. Nearly half of people with cancer report a lot of distress.

People with the same diagnosis can feel very different levels of distress. Distress tends to be lower when the demands feel manageable and support is high. That is why practical help, like managing side effects or sorting out costs, can also ease the mind.

Each person copes in their own way, often shaped by personality and upbringing. There is no single right way.

## Coping skills change along the way

Different moments ask for different kinds of strength:

- **Waiting for tests and hearing the diagnosis.** Worry often starts before the diagnosis. It's normal to find it hard to take in what doctors say. Ask for things to be written down, and bring someone with you.
- **During treatment.** Small changes help, such as adjusting your daily routine or work around treatment days. These are real coping skills.
- **When treatment ends.** Relief can come with new anxiety, especially as visits to the care team become less frequent. Worry before follow-up visits and while waiting for results is very common.
- **If the cancer comes back, or the goal of treatment changes** to comfort and relief of symptoms, distress often rises again. Many people find hope in meaningful activities, relationships, and faith or spirituality.
- **Long after treatment.** Some people live with fear of the cancer returning, tiredness, or reminders that bring back the hard times. Many also say they came to value life, and what matters most, more deeply.

## Things that help

People who adjust well often keep finding meaning in their lives. These are some ways to support that:

- **Keep some routine.** Carry on with usual activities and work where you can, and keep doing things that matter to you.
- **Express your feelings.** Talking to family, friends, others who have had cancer, or a counsellor helps many people let go of strong feelings. Writing in a diary can help if you'd rather not talk.
- **Stay connected.** People who withdraw from others are more likely to feel hopeless. Even short calls or visits count.
- **Learn what you need to know.** Understanding your situation often makes it feel less frightening. See [Understanding Cancer: The Basics](/guides/understanding-cancer-basics).
- **Find small good moments.** A favourite meal, music, time with a child, prayer, being outdoors. These don't fix anything, but they make the days more liveable.
- **Draw on faith or spirituality**, if it is part of your life.
- **Move a little**, if your doctor says it's okay. Regular activity and counselling can help with long-term worries and tiredness after treatment.

## Help from professionals works

Studies have found that people who are struggling to adjust are helped by:

- counselling or talk therapy
- relaxation training
- education sessions about cancer
- support groups
- therapy that helps with questions of meaning and life's limits

Benefits include less depression and anxiety, fewer symptoms, and feeling more hopeful.

**Ask your hospital** whether it has a psychologist, psychiatrist, counsellor, social worker, palliative care specialist or religious counsellor you can talk to. These are the kinds of professionals who help people having trouble coping with cancer.

### When to ask for help

Talk to your doctor if distress is getting in the way of daily life, at home, at work or in relationships, or if sadness lasts more than two weeks. Sometimes counselling is combined with medicine. See the signs of depression in [Processing the Diagnosis](/guides/processing-the-diagnosis#sadness-and-when-it-might-be-depression).

> **If you are thinking of hurting yourself, please reach out now.** Call **Tele-MANAS on 14416**, free, any time, or **112** in an emergency.

## Realistic hope

Hope doesn't have to mean expecting a particular outcome. What you hope for can change over time: for a good day tomorrow, for less pain, for time with family, for peace. Hope like this is honest, and it is always available.

## For caregivers

Everything here applies to you too. Caregivers often carry high distress quietly. Use the same tools, accept help, and see our guide on [supporting a loved one](/guides/supporting-a-loved-one).
