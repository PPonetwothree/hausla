Not knowing what will happen is often the hardest part of starting treatment. This guide describes, in general terms, what the three most common treatments involve. **Your own treatment may be different. Your care team's instructions always come first.**

> **Contact your care team straight away** if you have a fever of 38 °C (100.5 °F) or higher, or chills, during treatment. See [signs of infection](#infection-know-the-warning-signs) below.

## Before treatment starts

Ask your care team, and write the answers down:

- What exactly will be done, where, and how long will it take?
- How should I prepare? Is there anything I should not eat, drink or take beforehand?
- What side effects are likely, and which ones mean I should contact you immediately?
- **Who do I call, at what number, including at night and on holidays?**

Keep that number with you and give a copy to your family.

## Surgery

In cancer surgery, a surgeon removes the cancer from the body, usually along with a little healthy tissue around it. Sometimes nearby lymph nodes are also removed and checked under a microscope to see whether the cancer has spread, which helps doctors plan what comes next.

**Before surgery**

- You may need tests, such as blood tests, a chest X-ray and a heart tracing (ECG).
- You may be told not to eat or drink for a period beforehand. **Follow these instructions exactly**; if you don't, the surgery may have to be postponed.

**During surgery**, medicines called **anaesthesia** keep you from feeling pain. Depending on the operation, they numb a small area, a larger part of the body, or put you fully to sleep.

**After surgery**

- Most people have some **pain** in the area operated on. Pain can be managed: talk about pain control before the operation, and tell your nurse or doctor if pain is not controlled afterwards.
- **Infection** can happen. Follow the nurse's instructions for looking after the wound.
- Other risks include bleeding, damage to nearby tissue and reactions to anaesthesia. Ask your surgeon about the risks of your particular operation.

Before you go home, make sure you know how to control pain, what you should and shouldn't do, how to care for the wound, how to spot infection, and when to come back. There is usually a follow-up visit with the surgeon a week or two later.

## Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy uses medicines to kill cancer cells or stop them growing. It may be the only treatment, but most often it is combined with others: to shrink a tumour before surgery or radiotherapy, to destroy cancer cells that may remain afterwards, or to treat cancer that has returned or spread.

**How it is given.** Most often through a thin needle into a vein in the hand or arm (an "IV" or drip). It can also be given as tablets, as an injection, or in other ways. Some people have a thin tube (a **catheter**) or a small disc under the skin (a **port**) placed in a large vein so that the vein doesn't need a new needle each time.

**Cycles.** Chemotherapy is often given in cycles: a period of treatment followed by a period of rest, which gives the body a chance to recover. Schedules vary widely from person to person.

**Don't skip a session** unless your doctor tells you to. Sometimes doctors change the schedule because of side effects; they will tell you when to start again.

**How will you feel?** People respond very differently, so even your doctors cannot say for sure. The most common side effect is **tiredness** (fatigue). You can prepare by:

- asking someone to travel with you to and from chemotherapy
- planning to rest on the day of treatment and the day after
- asking for help with meals and children on those days

**Why other side effects happen.** Chemotherapy also affects healthy cells that grow quickly, such as those lining the mouth and gut and those that make hair grow. This can cause mouth sores, nausea and hair loss. **These side effects often get better or go away after chemotherapy ends.**

**How will we know it's working?** You will see your doctor often. They will examine you and order tests and scans. You cannot tell whether chemotherapy is working from its side effects: strong side effects don't mean it is working well, and having none doesn't mean it isn't.

## Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy uses high doses of radiation to kill cancer cells or slow their growth. It does not work instantly: it takes days or weeks of treatment before cancer cells start to die, and they keep dying for weeks or months after treatment ends.

There are two main types:

- **External beam radiotherapy.** A large machine aims radiation at the cancer. It may be noisy and it moves around you, but **it does not touch you**. It treats only one part of the body; for example, for lung cancer, only the chest.
- **Internal radiotherapy.** A source of radiation is placed inside the body, in or near the tumour (called **brachytherapy**), or given as a liquid that you swallow or receive through a vein. With these, your body may give off radiation for a while, and your care team will explain any precautions for you and your family.

Radiotherapy can also affect healthy cells near the area being treated, which is what causes side effects. They depend on which part of the body is treated. Ask your team what to expect for your treatment.

Radiotherapy is also used to ease symptoms, for example to shrink a tumour that is causing pain.

## Side effects: speak up

Side effects happen when treatment affects healthy tissue. They vary from person to person, **even among people having the same treatment**.

Common ones include tiredness, nausea and vomiting, hair loss, mouth and throat problems, diarrhoea or constipation, skin changes, pain, and trouble sleeping. Some treatments can affect fertility; if having children in future matters to you, ask about this **before** treatment starts.

**Tell your care team about every side effect.** Many can be prevented, treated or reduced. Putting up with them silently doesn't help your treatment.

### Infection: know the warning signs

Some cancers and treatments, especially chemotherapy, lower the white blood cells that fight infection. Infections during cancer treatment can be life-threatening and need urgent attention.

**Contact your care team straight away if you have:**

- **a fever of 38 °C (100.5 °F) or higher**, or chills
- a cough or sore throat
- diarrhoea
- ear pain, headache, or a stiff or sore neck
- a skin rash
- sores or a white coating in your mouth or on your tongue
- swelling or redness, especially where a catheter or port enters your body
- blood in your urine, cloudy urine, or pain when passing urine

**Ask your doctor or nurse before taking any medicine for a fever**, even paracetamol. It can bring the temperature down but hide the signs of a more serious problem.

**To lower the risk:** wash your hands often and well with soap and water, especially before eating, and ask the people around you to do the same. Keep any catheter area clean and dry, look after your teeth and mouth, and clean cuts and scrapes well.

## The days in between

- Keep a simple diary: how you feel, what you ate, any side effects, questions for the next visit.
- Bring your file and medicine list to every visit.
- Rest when you need to, and accept help.
- If you are staying away from home for treatment, see [Coming to Delhi for treatment](/find-care/coming-to-delhi).
- It is normal for mood to rise and fall during treatment. See [Building Mental Strength & Resilience](/guides/building-mental-strength).
